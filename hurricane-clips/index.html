The roof leaves before the walls do.

Hurricane Clips · Roof to wall, and what it is worth
Florida General Contractor · CGC1525413

Wind does not push a roof off. It lifts it. Your roof weighs seven to ten pounds a square foot, and a hurricane pulls up on it four to five times that hard.

At the corners and edges it is worse: ten to fifteen times. Florida’s own retrofit guide says to picture the house upside down and ask what is holding it together.

The answer is whatever ties your trusses to your walls. In a lot of older Tampa Bay homes that is three nails driven at an angle.

0 2 · The Load Path·IBHS · FEMA · Florida DEM

Wind looks for the weakest link.

It finds it at the top of the wall.

GAF describes them plainly: metal fasteners that connect your home’s roof trusses to its walls. A continuous load path carries wind loads from the roof through the walls into the ground, instead of into one connection. IBHS calls it the structural backbone of a house.

Here is the arithmetic, from Florida’s own Hurricane Retrofit Guide. Take a thirty foot wide roof under fifty pounds per square foot of uplift. Subtract the roof’s own weight and you are left with about six hundred pounds of net lift on every linear foot of wall.

Trusses sit two feet apart. So each single connection has to hold roughly twelve hundred pounds. That is what your roof-to-wall connection is being asked for, and it is why IBHS says roof damage drives an estimated seventy to ninety percent of insured residential catastrophe losses.

Your home’s ability to resist extreme wind forces is only as effective as its weakest link.Federal Alliance for Safe Homes
0 3 · The Five Grades·Form OIR-B1-1802

Toe nails to double wraps.

Every step up is a stronger hold and a better rating.

An inspector grades the weakest connection they can find, not the best one, so one bad bay sets the grade for the whole house. Florida requires connectors rated at least five hundred pounds on new work, which against a twelve hundred pound demand is a floor rather than a comfortable margin.

Five grades, and the real thing.

Nobody should have to guess what a single wrap is.

The drawings come first because photographs of these four connections side by side are almost impossible to tell apart: it is six inches of galvanised steel in a dark attic behind blown insulation. Drawn, the difference is obvious, and the difference is the whole argument.

In every drawing the sloped board is your roof, the block below it is your wall, and the metal is what holds the two together. The photographs after them are the same connections in the open, before a ceiling went in.

The connectors come from Simpson Strong-Tie, who have made hurricane ties since 1972 and joist hangers since 1956. They are a public company, they run their own accredited wind and seismic test laboratories, and every part carries a published, tested uplift rating rather than a claim.

What the form actually says.

The five classes, and the number each one has to hold.

Your insurer does not read a brochure. They read a grade on a state form, and the grade comes from what an inspector can see and document. These are the definitions that decide it.

0 6 · Your Questions·Retrofit · cost · grants

When this is actually worth doing.

The honest answer is: while somebody is already up there.

This is rarely a job on its own. Getting to the connection means going into the attic, pulling soffits, or cutting the deck back at the eave, and most of the cost is that access. During a re-roof the access is already open and the labour overlaps, which is why the Federal Alliance for Safe Homes says plainly that a re-roof is the easiest time to do it.

Florida forces the issue on some houses anyway. When a roof covering is replaced on a building in a wind-borne debris region valued at $300,000 or more, roof-to-wall connections must be improved, capped at fifteen percent of the cost of the re-roof.

  • So what am I actually buying?
    A better grade on the state wind mitigation form, which is what your insurer prices the wind portion of your premium from. The metal is cheap. What you are paying for is reaching it and documenting it.
  • How much does it cost?
    The National Association of Home Builders puts professional installation on a typical 2,500 square foot home at roughly $2,300 to $3,600, with materials alone around $350 to $800. That is a national figure, not a Florida quote, and it drops a lot if we are already re-roofing.
  • Does this actually work in a real storm?
    After Hurricane Michael came ashore near 160 mph, IBHS assessed 186 homes in the area. The ten built to the FORTIFIED standard, which requires these connections, were among the last houses standing.
  • Will it lower my insurance?
    It is one of the lines a carrier calculates a wind credit from, and it is widely regarded as one of the most valuable. Credits are filed per carrier, so ask yours what moving from clips to wraps is worth on your policy.
  • Can you even reach it on my house?
    Sometimes not. Truss geometry, blown insulation and blocked soffits all get in the way, and on some homes the honest answer is that it cannot be done without opening things we should not open. That answer comes after somebody has been in the attic.
  • Is this a do-it-yourself job?
    No, and GAF says so in as many words. The fasteners are specified by the manufacturer, the nail pattern is what the rating depends on, and a building inspector has to approve the work. Getting it almost right earns you nothing on the form.
  • What changed on the form in 2026?
    It now recognises performance-based retrofits. A connector rated to the tested uplift threshold and installed to the manufacturer’s instructions is graded on that rating rather than on its shape.
  • Are there grants?
    My Safe Florida Home funds roof-to-wall attachment at two state dollars per homeowner dollar up to ten thousand, with no match required for qualifying low-income applicants. It runs in funding rounds, so check whether it is open.
  • Do I need a new roof to do this?
    No. But if a re-roof is coming, do it then. That is the whole recommendation, and it is why we raise it during a roof conversation rather than selling it on its own.

How a retrofit actually goes.

Three stages, and an inspector at the end of them.

GAF sets out the same sequence: assessment, installation, inspection. What follows is what that means on a Tampa Bay house.

  1. 0 1

    Somebody gets in the attic

    We find out what you have, whether it can be reached, and what grade an inspector would give it today. On some houses this ends with us telling you it is not worth doing.

  2. 0 2

    Access, and this is the cost

    From inside the attic, from the soffit, or from above during a re-roof by cutting the decking back at the eave and patching it. The metal is cheap. Reaching it is not.

  3. 0 3

    Connectors fitted to spec

    The manufacturer’s fastener in the manufacturer’s hole pattern, at every rafter. The rating only exists if it is installed the way it was tested.

  4. 0 4

    Inspection and paperwork

    A building inspector signs off. Then a wind mitigation inspection puts the new grade on form OIR-B1-1802, which is the document your insurer actually prices from.
